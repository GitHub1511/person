## 17. Complexity levels, the ocular system, the inner world, and a generative behaviour space

All components described in this section were added after §1-§16, with the
aim of substantially enlarging the internal world of the person and of
increasing the variety of its possible behaviour.  The whole of this material
is *toy* modelling with plausible orders of magnitude; none of it is claimed to
correspond to a real mammal (a mouse possesses orders of magnitude more
receptors than any level considered here).

### 17.1 A single scaling parameter: `complexity.py`

Every quantity that scales is read from a single `Complexity` profile, **once
at import time** (the skin geometry, and therefore the MuJoCo model, is
generated from it).  The level is selected with
`PERSON_COMPLEXITY=base|rich|extreme|max` (or a JSON file of overrides), or
persistently with `tools/scale_complexity.py --set LEVEL`.  The default level
is `extreme`.

| knob | base | rich | extreme | max |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| skin density (linear; taxels grow as its square) | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| extra per-taxel tactile channels | no | yes | yes | yes |
| spindles / Golgi tendon organs (GTOs) per muscle | 1 / 1 | 8 / 4 | 16 / 8 | 32 / 16 |
| canal afferents per canal / otolith hair cells per organ | 1 / 1 | 24 / 64 | 64 / 192 | 128 / 384 |
| cochlear bands per ear | 24 | 96 | 192 | 384 |
| olfactory receptor types / taste cell types | 24 / 5 | 160 / 24 | 320 / 48 | 640 / 96 |
| retina (w x h) | 48x36 | 72x54 | 96x72 | 128x96 |
| corneal nerve units / tear-film sectors per eye | 16 / 16 | 96 / 48 | 192 / 96 | 384 / 192 |
| neural-mass units | 512 | 4,096 | 12,288 | 32,768 |
| motor units per muscle | 4 | 16 | 32 | 64 |
| chemistry analytes | 48 | 240 | 480 | 960 |
| circadian oscillators / episodic memory slots | 16 / 256 | 96 / 2,048 | 192 / 4,096 | 384 / 8,192 |
| inner world on | no | yes | yes | yes |

(The full table is printed by `python tools/scale_complexity.py --list`.)  At
`base`, the person corresponds to the original person described in §2-§11.

**Not yet measured:** the total number of sensory scalars, the number of state
variables, the memory per instance and the milliseconds per step at
`rich`/`extreme`/`max`.  The command
`python tools/scale_complexity.py --measure rich extreme` builds each level in
a separate process and reports these quantities; this run has not been
completed, and this README therefore reports no figures for it.

### 17.2 Increased skin density and receptor counts

* `skin.default_patches()` multiplies the taxel grid of each patch by the skin
  density (`base` retains the original 1,872 taxels).
* `receptors.py` adds 17 extended tactile channels per taxel
  (`EXTENDED_TACTILE_CHANNELS`), with sensitisation and fatigue modifiers
  supplied by the inner world, together with the `ReceptorFrame.ext`
  dictionary for the additional banks.
* `senses_ext.py` generates *populations* of cells (spindles and Golgi tendon
  organs, vestibular canal and otolith hair cells for both ears, a two-ear
  cochlea, olfactory glomeruli, taste cells, and a retina bank), each
  modelled as a thresholded, saturating, adapting unit with randomised
  parameters.

### 17.3 Ocular drying and blinking (`ocular.py`)

Each eye is modelled with a tear film that thins and evaporates, a meniscus
reservoir, lipid and mucin layers, and a population of corneal nerve terminals
(cold, polymodal nociceptor and lid-friction mechanoreceptor classes).  The
*sensation of dryness* drives a blink central pattern generator and
additionally propagates to the remainder of the person: dryness blurs vision,
adds discomfort to affect, sensitises pain, and contributes an `ocular_comfort`
drive and an urge to rub the eyes; staring suppresses blinking (which dries the
eyes further); speaking, arousal and tiredness alter the blink rate; sadness
produces tears; dehydration dries the eyes; and the lids droop with
sleepiness.  The eyes and lids are visible in the model (visual-only geoms
moved at runtime).  The stated orders of magnitude (tear film ~3 um, break-up
time 10-20 s, ~4-15 blinks per minute) are plausible; the model is not a
clinical one.

### 17.4 The inner world (`inner_organs.py`, `inner_brain.py`, `inner_world.py`)

The inner world is added to the original 67 interoceptive variables, 28
emotions, 25 neuromodulators and 16 drives (all of which remain present and
continue to drive the system as a whole):

* **Organs:** vascular beds, lungs (alveolar units), kidney (nephron groups),
  liver zones, gut segments with a microbiome, ~3,300 motor units with fatigue
  and soreness, per-patch skin thermoregulation, an immune network with
  cytokines and tissue damage/healing, and a 480-channel blood/tissue
  chemistry panel (a factor model with ~70 mechanistic channels).
* **Brain side:** a circadian clock (~200 coupled oscillators plus sleep
  pressure), a ~12,000-unit neural mass in 20 populations with neuromodulated
  gain, episodic memory with similarity-based recall, Rescorla-Wagner
  conditioning, and an interoceptive prediction model with learned precision.
* **Coupling:** the inner world modulates skin blood flow, sweat and itch, as
  well as affect (a threat tone, rumination, interoceptive surprise), and
  contributes five additional drives (ocular comfort, muscle soreness, gut
  discomfort, mental fatigue, urge to shift), behavioural desire, and two new
  blocks of the latent vector ("ocular", 16 numbers, and "inner", 128
  numbers).

Each organ model exhibits the appropriate *qualitative* couplings (exertion
recruits and fatigues motor units, heat opens skin beds and initiates
sweating, damage drives inflammation, which sensitises skin) and is otherwise
toy physiology.

### 17.5 A generative behaviour space (`behavior_space.py`, `behavior_exec.py`)

The 55 hand-written policies of §8 no longer constitute the entire repertoire.
A *behaviour* is now represented as a descriptor: one choice in each of 33
factored channels (head, eyes, lids and blink pattern, mouth and
vocalisation, torso, one of 48 arm schemas with a +-2 step variation on each
of six joints per arm, hand shapes, stance, style, duration, object intent,
self-touch on 16 regions, walking, ...).  Per arm there are hundreds of
thousands of configurations, and the full descriptor space is on the order of
10^37.  This figure enumerates *describable* descriptors, not distinct
meaningful behaviours.  Every descriptor compiles to an action that the body
can execute.

* `BehaviorSelector` converts drives, affect and the state of the eyes into a
  desire over affinity tags, samples candidates from this structured prior,
  and scores them by expected free energy using the learned forward model,
  with a repetition penalty and a balance guard.
* `BehaviorExecutor` executes the chosen behaviour at 50 Hz (joint targets
  with speed/amplitude/tremor/rhythm, gaze, blink patterns, jaw, weight shift
  and crouch, reaching, grasping and self-touch).
* Two modes are provided: **autonomous** (these behaviours constitute the
  whole of its activity) and **ambient** (the language-model mind is
  responsible for deliberate action, and this layer supplies involuntary
  behaviour on the channels that the mind is not using: glances, shifting,
  blinking, and a hand movement toward a dry eye).  The mind may also invoke
  `express(...)`.

### 17.6 Learning to use its own body (`body_learning.py`, `tools/train_body.py`)

Most descriptors are benign, whereas a subset causes the body to lose balance.
`BodySafety` is a logistic model over the components of a behaviour (together
with a few continuous summaries, such as the deviation of the arms from rest)
that predicts the probability that the behaviour unbalances the body.  It is
fitted offline from many bodies performing motor babbling in parallel
(`tools/train_body.py`: 8 workers, three rounds, 1,814 training behaviours;
held-out area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of
**0.777**) and continues to adapt online from the outcome of each behaviour.
The selector adds the predicted hazard to the expected free energy of a
candidate.

Evaluation was performed on fresh copies of the person under an identical
budget, for 24 simulated minutes each, at `base` complexity, because the
balance physics does not depend on receptor counts:

| | behaviours | unsafe per 100 | falls | falls per simulated hour | distinct |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| unconstrained babbling | 463 | 45.1 | 123 | 307 | 100 % |
| with the learned safety model | 465 | 11.8 | 35 | 87 | 99 % |

The number of falls per hour decreased by about 72 % and variety was
preserved; however, **the person still falls about 87 times per simulated
hour**; a model with an AUC of 0.78 is a useful filter rather than a
solution.  The weights are stored in
`embodied_human/body_safety.json`; the report is available as
`out/train_body_report.json`.

### 17.7 Limitations of this part

* **Not verified at scale:** no completed end-to-end run at `rich`/`extreme`
  has been performed, no sensor/state/memory/speed measurements have been
  obtained, and no `run_sim.py` regression has been run after these additions.
* The resource cost is substantial: each simulated person requires roughly
  1-1.5 GB of random-access memory (RAM), so that on a 32 GB machine with
  other applications running, only a small number of instances can be executed
  in parallel.
* The behaviour space is wide, but each behaviour is a motion template rather
  than a discovered skill; the figure "~10^37" enumerates descriptors.
* The ocular, organ and neural-mass models are plausible toy models.
* The learned safety model was evaluated only at `base`, with random babbling,
  and not over a long autonomous lifetime.
* **Olfaction and taste are currently degraded** as a consequence of the two
  helper steps described in §18.1 (the original object-based olfaction and
  gustation were removed; the new sources exist only at `rich`).  Prior to
  this change, they were in any case only weakly grounded, since the scene
  contains few odorous objects.
